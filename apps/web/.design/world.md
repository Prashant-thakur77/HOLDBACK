# HOLDBACK web: world board

What already exists in the subject's own world, collected before looking at any website.
Every tradition below was studied from photographs of real objects, and every colour was
sampled from a photograph (median of the ink or paper pixels in a marked crop), not invented.
Photographs shift with light and age, so the hexes are starting points to tune on screen.

## Place and people

HOLDBACK has no single place. Its world is **the counter**: every spot where money changes
hands slowly enough for someone to make a mark about it.

- The bus aisle, where a conductor punches a paper ticket and tears it from the pad.
- The post office window, where a clerk strikes a date stamp on a money order, and where
  unclaimed money goes back to the sender.
- The bank passbook window, where each entry is one ruled line.
- The cheque, crossed by hand with two lines so it cannot be cashed on the spot.
- The lottery stand and the pools counter of Latin America, where a slip is valid until a
  printed date.
- The corner shop with a payment code taped to the counter and a speaker that says each
  payment aloud.
- Before clocks, the timekeeper's pot: a copper bowl with a pinhole floating on water, and a
  person watching it who strikes a gong when it sinks.

People: conductor, postal clerk, cashier, the merchant's bookkeeper, notary, lottery seller,
shopkeeper, and the person sending money home.

## Visual traditions

### 1. The conductor's punched bus ticket (western India)

Seen: four tickets from state, city and municipal operators, plus card rail tickets.

- One ink per ticket (red, magenta, blue or black) on thin pale stock. The ink colour tells the
  ticket type; it is never decoration. At most one overprint in a second ink for the fare.
- A border rule, then a full grid of equal cells with running numerals in Devanagari, set as
  two mirrored halves (outward and return) around a centre block.
- The centre block breaks the grid: the fare in large numerals with its unit, in a heavier
  face. Arrows mark direction.
- The serial number sits alone at the top in a numbering-machine face, black, never quite
  aligned with the rest.
- One round hole punched through a cell records the decision. The bottom edge is torn, not
  cut. A line of small print runs along the foot ("issued under the rules, not transferable").
- Never: photographs, tints, gradients, empty space, more than two inks.

### 2. The lottery ticket and the pools slip (Mexico, Brazil)

Seen: national lottery tickets of 1951 and 1960; football pools slips of 1972.

- Two flat inks plus black numbering. Panels of solid ink with the lettering reversed out.
- The number is the hero: large black machine digits, repeated underneath in words.
- Corner cartouches for series and fraction; the draw date written out in full as a banner;
  an expiry line ("expires in a year").
- An engraved-style vignette on one side only; a fine tint pattern behind the prize figure,
  whose numerals are outlined.
- The pools slip: one ink on buff stock, numbered rows of three boxes marked with a hand-drawn
  X, a serial in a circle.
- Never: lowercase body text, white space left unused, a clean machine-cut edge on all sides.

### 3. The hundi and the promissory note (India, Burma)

Seen: an accepted hundi of 1903 with its agency handstamp; notes of 1926 and 1939.

- A printed sentence with gaps completed by hand ("On demand we promise to pay ... the sum of
  Rupees ... only"). Blackletter for the two words that carry the promise.
- The amount twice: figures at top left, underlined; words in the sentence, closed with
  "only" and a ruled dash so nothing can be added.
- Revenue stamps pasted in a row or column and cancelled by signing or stamping across them.
- Oval and boxed handstamps in violet, struck at an angle over the text. A boxed stamp is a
  small form of its own: number, date, drawer, drawee, filled in by hand.
- When paid, CANCELLED is struck diagonally across the face.
- Never: a stamp that sits square to the page, or a stamp without a number or date.

### 4. Postal date stamps, money orders and return marks

Seen: circular date stamps from 1904 to 2021; covers returned to sender; money order forms
and cards from the 1870s to the 1900s; a rubber stamp beside its own impression; a stamp-pad
tin.

- Circular date stamp: one or two rings, the place name on the curve, the date in straight
  lines across the middle, sometimes wavy bars to one side.
- One ink (black, red or violet), never even: heavy on one side, broken where the rubber did
  not touch, letters filling in where it was over-inked.
- A return mark is a boxed word or a two-line handstamp in a second colour, struck beside the
  address, with a pointing direction. It never covers the original writing.
- A money order is a form in two or three parts divided by rules or perforations: the order,
  the advice, the receipt. A cancelled one carries the word struck diagonally, more than once.
- Never: perfectly round, perfectly inked, or undated.

### 5. The savings passbook and the savings certificate

Seen: a passbook of 1971 from Sao Paulo; a post office deposit book with a date stamp beside
every entry; post office savings certificates of 1953 and 1956; a savings envelope from Lima.

- Ruled columns: date, entry, balance. Printed headings in small capitals; entries typed or
  written; one round date stamp per line in its own column.
- A boxed handstamp in magenta struck over the page for special conditions.
- Certificates: one ink (violet-blue or salmon), a patterned border with the denomination in
  all four corners, and on the back a table of what the paper is worth after each year.
- Punched holes and date stamps record each event in the life of the paper.
- Never: illustration inside the table. Ornament lives only in the border.

### 6. Fiscal stamps cancelled by punching (India, 1860s and 1870s)

Seen: court fee and special adhesive stamps.

- One ink per value; an oval portrait frame inside dense line ornament; the value in the
  corners in two scripts.
- Cancelled by punching large round holes straight through the middle: the mark is made by
  removing material, so it cannot be cleaned off.
- Never: a second colour inside the frame.

### 7. The crossed cheque

Seen: a 1956 cheque with a hand-drawn crossing.

- A pale security tint as ground; the bank's name in red display capitals; the payee and
  amount written by hand in blue-violet ink.
- Two parallel diagonal strokes drawn by hand across the face. That is the whole instruction:
  it must go through an account, not over the counter.
- An embossed oval duty stamp at one end; the amount boxed at the right.

### 8. Cordel pamphlet covers (north-east Brazil)

Seen: a single-colour cover and a market display of pamphlets hung on strings.

- One ink (red or black) on cream or coloured stock. Figures are solid shapes with white cut
  lines, all on one plane, filling the frame.
- Title in letterpress capitals above, widely spaced; a narrow ornamental strip at each side.
- Studied for how to cut any picture in one ink. Not adopted as the art tradition: it is
  figurative storytelling with living makers, and this product needs marks, not characters.

### 9. Street lettering (Peru, Mexico, India)

Seen: fluorescent concert posters, a painted wall of slogans, a painted brick wall notice.

- Fluorescent pink, yellow and green letters with black outlines, shadows and highlights on
  tilted baselines; block capitals painted the full height of a wall; red and blue Devanagari
  on whitewash.
- Too loud for this project's calm. Kept only as a colour source and as a reminder of how
  large the important word should be.

### 10. The sinking-bowl clock

Seen: a hammered copper bowl with a pinhole floating in a tin basin; a clay version;
an engraving of bowls of several sizes.

- A shallow bowl of beaten copper, darker at the rim, with one small hole at the centre of
  the base. Rings spread from the hole as water comes in.
- It floats in a wider, plainer vessel. The bowl is always the warm colour, the basin the
  cool one.
- Time is read by how low the bowl sits. When it goes under, someone strikes a gong, lifts it
  out, empties it and sets it down again. In India one sinking was a ghati, about 24 minutes
  by museum and textbook accounts (the exact figure and its history vary by source).

### 11. The shop-counter payment placard

Seen: a printed payment-code placard as used at shop counters.

- A tall card: the wordmark large at the top, "accepted here", the square code in a white
  field, the rail's mark at the foot. Two flat colours and white.
- It is the newest object in this world and the one both audiences touch daily. Kept as
  context and as a colour source; its look belongs to its owners and is not borrowed.

Looked for and not found in usable photographs: fraud-warning posters and public-service
notices, and pawn tickets. Nothing in this document is drawn from them.

## Physical actions

1. **Punch** a hole through one cell of a ticket. The chad falls; the mark is permanent.
2. **Tear** a ticket from the pad, or a receipt from its counterfoil, along a perforation.
3. **Strike** a date stamp: press on the pad, press on the paper, rock once, lift.
4. **Cross** a cheque: two parallel strokes across the face, drawn by hand.
5. **Float** the bowl, and **lift it out** before it goes under.
6. **Sound** the gong when the bowl sinks.
7. **Rule off** an amount: write "only", then draw the dash that stops anything being added.
8. **Mark** a box with an X on a slip.
9. **Paste** a revenue stamp and sign across its edge.
10. **Hand back**: slide the paper across the counter to the person it came from.

## Sounds, words and scripts

- **Sounds:** the two-part click of a ticket punch; a stamp's thud on the pad and then the
  paper; a single gong; paper tearing along a perforation; the counter speaker announcing a
  payment in a flat, clear voice.
- **Hindi (Devanagari, with Devanagari numerals where a grid calls for them):** वापस लें
  (take it back), रोकें (hold), रसीद (receipt), मियाद (term, the fixed period of a dated
  hundi), दर्शनी (payable at sight), घड़ी (clock, from the ghati of the bowl clock), सावधान
  (careful), धोखाधड़ी (fraud), अहस्तांतरणीय (not transferable, as printed on tickets).
- **Spanish:** deshacer, devolver, caduca (expires, as printed on lottery tickets), resguardo
  (stub), comprobante (receipt), giro (money order), cheque cruzado, estafa (scam).
- **Portuguese:** desfazer, estorno (a reversed payment), devolução, prazo (term), canhoto
  (counterfoil), comprovante, carimbo (stamp), golpe (scam).
- **English:** hold, undo window, clear, stop payment, counterfoil, return to sender.
- Type follows the objects: a numbering-machine face for serials and amounts, a plain heavy
  face for the fare block, small capitals for column heads. Local scripts are set as text,
  with their own numerals and line-height, never as ornament.

## The choice

**Art tradition: the one-ink punched ticket.** The conductor's bus ticket, with its Latin
American cousins the lottery ticket and the pools slip. A hold already is a ticket: it has a
serial (the hold's address), a fare (the amount), a grid of stages (the hours of the window),
an "expires" line (the release time) and exactly one decision recorded on it (the risk tier).
On a punched ticket that decision is made by removing paper, so it cannot be edited later,
which is what writing the evidence hash on-chain does. The grid is set in the reader's own
numerals, so switching language changes the drawing itself, not a caption under it. No
payments or chain site draws this way; boarding passes and thermal receipts are common, a
punched stage grid is not.

**Action: lift the bowl before it sinks.** The bowl clock is a timer made from a hole, like
the ticket. A smaller hole means a longer float, which is exactly how the risk agent sizes a
window: the more dangerous the recipient, the smaller the hole it gives the bowl. Time is the
bowl filling. Release is the bowl going under, with one gong. Undo is a hand lifting it out,
at any moment before that. It is the only clock whose count a person ends by picking it up,
and that is the product's one tap.

**Swap test.** Put another product's name on a punched hour grid whose hole was placed by a
risk agent, beside a bowl whose pinhole was sized by scam evidence, and neither makes sense.
They belong to a payment that can be taken back for a measured time.

**Respect.** Tickets, stamps and forms are drawn as originals that follow the grammar above.
No operator's name, emblem or layout is copied, no postal or state mark is imitated, and no
named maker's work is reproduced. The bowl clock was used in temples as well as courts and
markets; it is drawn here as an instrument, with no sacred imagery around it.
